Step 4 in detail: where the ceiling comes from

Step 3 needs a ceiling: a weighted count of primes that never rises above about x½ + 9ε on long windows. The announced proof gets it from arithmetic, in six moves. Panels marked real data use actual primes and the proof's actual parameters; schematic panels show the shape of an idea. This follows the account in riemann-rev; the proof is machine-checked but not yet refereed.

STEP 4a schematic

Divisibility caps size

A whole number that is not zero and is divisible by many primes cannot be small: it is at least their product. Flip that around: if you know a number is small, only so many primes can divide it.

That is the only source of an upper bound in the whole argument.

Read more

The proof uses a lattice version, Bost's slope inequality, a tool of Arakelov theory. Picture a lattice as a grid of points with integer coordinates; its slope measures how finely its points are packed. If an injective integral map sends lattice A into lattice B and stretches nothing by more than a factor C, then slope(A) ≤ max slope(B) + log C. Divisibility by many primes pushes the slope of A up; the size of the map caps it. (Archive: slopeInequality_of, checked on its own.)

STEP 4b real data

Inert primes supply the divisibility

The proof works in an imaginary quadratic field ℚ(√−d): the ordinary numbers with √−d added. Its whole numbers look like a + b√−d. An ordinary prime p can behave in three ways there:

splits: factors into two new primes  ·  inert: stays prime  ·  ramifies: becomes a square

The quadratic character χ records which: +1, −1 or 0. The numbers the proof builds are divisible at the inert primes. Adding up what each one contributes gives ASK.

Read more: splitting, inert and ramified primes

In the Gaussian integers a + bi (the field ℚ(i)):

pwhat happensχ(p)
5, 13splits: 5 = (2 + i)(2 − i), 13 = (3 + 2i)(3 − 2i)+1
3, 7, 11inert: cannot be factored−1
2ramifies: 2 = −i·(1 + i)²0

For ℚ(i) the rule is: p splits exactly when p = a² + b², which happens when p ≡ 1 (mod 4). In general, p splits in ℚ(√−d) when −d is a square mod p, stays inert when it is not, and ramifies when p divides the field's discriminant. χ(p) is the Kronecker symbol (−d/p), a Dirichlet character like those in the explorer's L(s,χ) tab; the (−4/·) preset there is the character of ℚ(i). In the long run half the primes split and half stay inert. The general theory is the splitting of primes in number fields.

All nine fields in the menu have class number 1: unique factorization still holds, so every split prime factors into two explicit numbers of the field. Hover over a prime to see its factorization.

Why inert primes give divisibility. The numbers come from theta functions of fields with complex multiplication. At an inert prime, a Frobenius congruence makes certain 2×2 determinants divisible by √p (archive: key_minor_dvd, checked on its own).

Read more: what ASK is

ASK adds up the divisibility gained, in logarithmic units (the archive's ASK and MAIN):

ASK(a) = Σn ≤ a Λ(n) · (1 − χ(n)) · (a/n − 1)

  • Λ(n) = log p when n is a prime p or a power of it, else 0. It measures divisibility in log units, the way log(2·3·5) = log 2 + log 3 + log 5 in step 4a.
  • 1 − χ(n) keeps the inert primes: it is 2 for inert, 0 for split, 1 for ramified. For prime powers χ(pᵏ) = χ(p)ᵏ, so an inert p counts but p² does not.
  • a/n − 1 is the ramp: small primes count more, and nothing beyond a counts.

Set the ramp length to 12 or less to see the sum worked out term by term.

STEP 4c real data

Size gives MAIN, and ASK − MAIN is a prime sum

The size of the numbers can be bounded from above. Its expected value is MAIN. Subtract, and everything large cancels:

ASK − MAIN = G(a) − gχ(a)

the ζ prime sum of step 2 minus its twist by χ. Divisibility cannot exceed what the size allows, so this prime sum is capped from above. That is the one-sided bound step 3 needs.

Read more: what MAIN is

MAIN(a) = a · (log a − 1 − γ + L′/L(1, χ))

  • a(log a − 1 − γ) is the expected value of the ramp sum over all primes, the main term that G subtracts in step 2; γ ≈ 0.5772 is Euler's constant.
  • a · L′/L(1, χ) is the expected value of the twisted sum over χ, the main term of gχ. L′/L is the logarithmic derivative of the L-function of χ at s = 1; for ℚ(√−7) it is about 0.0156.

On the geometric side the same constant comes from the periods of the theta functions, through the Chowla–Selberg formula (archive: chowla_selberg_period). A prime-by-prime identity (ch_balance) and the cancellation of the main terms give ASK − MAIN = G − gχ exactly (here with equal weights). The chart computes ASK and MAIN from every prime up to 10⁶: they agree to about one part in a million, and the difference stays on the scale of √a.

The comparison that turns the geometric data into this prime-sum bound is checked in the archive as an implication (core_gm), with its geometric inputs stated as hypotheses.

STEP 4d real parameters

A chain of about √x theta lifts

One application of 4b–4c gives a bound that is too weak. The proof repeats the construction m times, in a chain of theta lifts between unitary groups.

The error then has two terms, one like x/m and one like m. Their sum is smallest at m = √x, and that is where the square root in the Riemann hypothesis comes from in this argument.

Read more

The source takes m between √(xδ) and 2√(xδ) with δ = x−ε/8, so m ≈ x½ − ε/16, slightly under √x. The comparison step demands x½ − ε ≤ m ≤ x½ + ε/2 (the shaded window), and the source's m always lies inside it. Denominators are tracked along the chain (archive: ChainInt).

riemann-rev reports the two cost terms but does not give an intuitive reason for each one's shape.

STEP 4e schematic

Removing the twist: averaging keeps only some characters

The bound from 4c mixes the wanted prime sum with an unwanted twist. The proof removes it with Fourier analysis on a finite abelian group, using its characters of a finite abelian group: average over a subgroup, and every character that is not identically 1 on that subgroup cancels out exactly.

What survives gets positive weights, which is what step 3's "no cancellation" condition needs.

Read more

The picture uses the cyclic group ℤ/12. Each row is a character χk; the hands show its values on the subgroup, and the bar shows their average, which is either 1 or exactly 0.

In the proof the group is (ℤ/2)² × A with a translation subgroup A₀. The kept characters that belong to the target carry the weights of the level; the other survivors ("discarded survivors") are bounded separately by 4o·x½+ε, using a square-root bound for finitely many specific characters. The fields have to be rebuilt at every scale x, with auxiliary primes and Behrend sets.

STEP 4f real budget

Paying for the losses: ½ + 9ε

Every step above loses a little: each loss is a factor x(something)·ε. Added up, they cost exactly 9ε, so the ceiling is x½ + 9ε.

The proof picks ε after the hypothetical zero, small enough that the ceiling stays below the zero's growth xβ. Then step 3 applies.

Read more

The staircase, from riemann-rev's exact bookkeeping: 13/8 for the worst cost term of the √x balance, 5 for the geometric error (C* = e_D + e_L + 3 with e_D = 2, e_L = 0), 1 for the density of the averaging set, 3/8 to absorb a constant 26, and 1 to restore the discarded characters.

Divisibility can also fall slightly short of the ideal. Galois cohomology along cyclotomic towers (Iwasawa theory, with Ferrero–Washington and Poitou–Tate duality) bounds that shortfall uniformly over a whole window; it enters the geometric error (archive: window1).

For a zero with real part β, the proof takes θ = ½ + ½·min(β − ½, ½) and any ε ≤ (θ − ½)/9.

Putting 4a–4f together: divisibility at inert primes (4b), weighed against size (4a, 4c), repeated about √x times (4d), cleaned of unwanted characters (4e) and paid for in ε (4f), caps the weighted prime count at x½ + 9ε on long windows. That is the ceiling of step 3.
