The zeta function

Why did Bernhard Riemann believe his hypothesis was true?#

He never said why. His 1859 paper mentions it in one sentence: he finds "approximately this number of real roots" and says it is "very probable that all roots are real", adding that he had set aside the search for a proof "after some fleeting futile attempts", because his main result did not need it. Three likely reasons:

  1. He computed zeros himself. In 1932 Carl Ludwig Siegel found, in Riemann's unpublished notes, careful calculations of the first zeros (the first is at t ≈ 14.1347), using a method now called the Riemann–Siegel formula. They sat exactly on the line.
  2. Zeros on the line are easy to count, and there seemed to be enough of them. On the line his function is real, so each zero is a sign change. He estimated the total number of zeros up to height T as about (T/2π)log(T/2π) − T/2π and found roughly that many sign changes.
  3. Symmetry. The functional equation makes the critical line the natural axis of the problem.

More than 1013 zeros have since been computed, all on the line. For the history, H. M. Edwards' Riemann's Zeta Function (1974) walks through the 1859 paper.

Is the zeta function symmetric across the line Re(s) = ½?#

ζ(s) itself is not. Its completed version ξ(s) is. The zeros in the critical strip are symmetric either way.

The functional equation links the two sides only up to a factor:

ζ(s) = χ(s)·ζ(1 − s),   χ(s) = 2sπs−1 sin(πs/2) Γ(1 − s)

That factor stretches one side, so |ζ| rises to the left and flattens toward 1 on the right. Riemann multiplied ζ by factors that cancel it:

ξ(s) = ½ s(s − 1) π−s/2 Γ(s/2) ζ(s),   with   ξ(s) = ξ(1 − s)  and  ξ(s̄) = conj ξ(s)

Together these give ξ(1 − s̄) = conj ξ(s). The point 1 − s̄ is the mirror image of s across the line, so |ξ| is exactly mirror-symmetric, and on the line itself ξ is real.

What does "conj ξ(s)" mean, and is the graph anti-symmetric across the real axis?#

conj ξ(s) is the complex conjugate of the value: if ξ(s) = a + bi, then conj ξ(s) = a − bi.

ξ(s̄) = conj ξ(s) says that reflecting the input across the real axis reflects the output. That is conjugate symmetry, not anti-symmetry:

Part of ξUnder t → −t
Re ξunchanged (symmetric)
Im ξchanges sign (anti-symmetric)
|ξ| (terrain height)unchanged
phase (color)θ → −θ

So the heights mirror exactly while paired colors swap. The same holds across the critical line. The two reflections together are a 180° rotation about s = ½, which is ξ(s) = ξ(1 − s).

Zeta and the primes

What is the relationship between the zeta function and the prime numbers?#

1. ζ is built from the primes. Euler showed, for Re(s) > 1:

ζ(s) = Σ 1/ns = ∏p 1/(1 − p−s)

Multiplying out the product gives every 1/ns exactly once, because every number factors into primes in one way.

2. The zeros determine where the primes are. Riemann's explicit formula is exact:

ψ(x) = x − Σρ xρ/ρ − log 2π − ½ log(1 − x−2)

The term x is the main trend, from the pole at s = 1. Each zero ρ = β + iγ adds a wave of size about xβ oscillating like cos(γ log x).

3. The hypothesis is about how regular the primes are. RH is equivalent to π(x) = li(x) + O(√x log x): the primes are as evenly spread as they can be. A zero with real part β > ½ would make the error grow like xβ.

What is the prime staircase?#

The graph of the prime-counting function π(x), the number of primes up to x. It is flat between primes and steps up by one at each prime: π(10) = 4, because of 2, 3, 5 and 7.

From far away it looks like the smooth curve x/log x, or more accurately li(x). Up close it wanders above and below. The Riemann hypothesis says that wandering stays about as small as √x.

The Primes tab draws a weighted version, ψ(x), whose steps are log p instead of 1 (see the next question).

What is the function ψ(x)?#

A weighted prime count. Each prime p up to x adds log p, and so does each prime power p², p³, …

For x = 10: the prime powers are 2, 3, 4, 5, 7, 8, 9, so ψ(10) = 3 log 2 + 2 log 3 + log 5 + log 7 = log 2520 ≈ 7.83. In general ψ(x) = log(lcm(1, 2, …, x)): 2520 is the smallest number divisible by 1 through 10.

Why log p? Near x, about one number in log x is prime, so weighting by log p cancels the thinning: ψ(x) ≈ x. (ψ(100) ≈ 94.05, ψ(1000) ≈ 996.68.) That makes ψ(x) − x the pure error.

Why prime powers? They are what ζ produces: −ζ′/ζ(s) = Σ Λ(n)/ns, with Λ(n) = log p at powers of p. With exactly these weights the explicit formula has no correction terms. Squares and higher powers contribute only about √x in total.

L-functions

What is the equivalent of the zeta function for L-functions?#

ζ is itself one: it is L(s, χ0) for the trivial character, every weight 1, modulus 1. A Dirichlet L-function reweights the terms with a character χ, a pattern that repeats every q steps and is multiplicative:

ζ(s)L(s, χ)
Σ 1/nsΣ χ(n)/ns
pole at s = 1no pole if χ is non-trivial; L(1, χ) ≠ 0 (Dirichlet's theorem)
ξ(s) = ξ(1 − s)Λ(s, χ) = ε(χ)·Λ(1 − s, χ̄), with |ε| = 1
trivial zeros −2, −4, …−𝔞, −𝔞 − 2, … (𝔞 = 0 even χ, 1 odd χ)
ψ(x): all primesψ(x; q, a): primes ≡ a mod q
RHGRH

For example, the zeta function of the Gaussian integers factors as ζ(s)·L(s, χ−4).

Why is the graph of L(s, χ) not symmetric?#

The raw L(s, χ) is not, just as ζ is not. Switch to the completed Λ(s, χ) view:

Real χComplex χ
mirror across σ = ½ (Λ view)yesyes
mirror across t = 0yesno: below the axis is the terrain of χ̄

For a complex χ, zeros above and below the axis are at different heights: for the order-4 character mod 5, at +6.18 and +8.46 but −4.13 and −9.44. Reflecting a zero of L(s, χ) gives a zero of L(s, χ̄), a different function. That is why a proof has to handle all characters together.

The announced proof

What is the gist of the proof? If a zero lies off the line, is the prime count estimate wrong?#

The classical part (von Koch, 1901): RH holds exactly when the prime count stays within about √x of its smooth trend. A zero at β > ½ would make it swing by about xβ, above and below, forever. (The prime number theorem stays true either way; it is the sharper error estimate that would fail.)

The new part, as the proof is described:

  1. Reflection. A zero left of the line has a twin to the right, so it is enough to rule out the right half.
  2. One side, some windows. It is enough to cap the upward swings of a positively weighted prime count at about √x, on infinitely many long windows [Y, Y3/2]. An off-line zero's growing wave would break through.
  3. The cap comes from arithmetic. A non-zero whole number divisible by many primes is large. Numbers built from theta functions are divisible at many primes and bounded in size; repeated about √x times, that gives the cap. The two costs x/m and m balance at m = √x.

What does it mean for a prime to split, stay inert, or ramify? And what is ASK?#

In a bigger number system an ordinary prime may factor. In the Gaussian integers a + bi:

pwhat happensχ(p)
5, 13splits: 5 = (2 + i)(2 − i)+1
3, 7, 11inert: stays prime−1
2ramifies: 2 = −i·(1 + i)²0

In ℚ(√−d), p splits when −d is a square mod p, stays inert when it is not, and ramifies when p divides the discriminant. The numbers the proof builds are divisible at inert primes. ASK adds up that divisibility:

ASK(a) = Σn ≤ a Λ(n)·(1 − χ(n))·(a/n − 1)

The factor 1 − χ(n) keeps inert primes (weight 2) and drops split ones. Against it stands the expected size MAIN, and ASK − MAIN is exactly a prime sum, G − gχ. Divisibility cannot exceed what size allows, so that prime sum is capped from above.

For a hypothetical zero at ½ + δ + iγ, when would it force the prime count outside the RH band, despite masking by the other zeros?#

Assume this quartet {ρ, ρ̄, 1 − ρ, 1 − ρ̄} is the only one off the line. Its main pair adds a wave of amplitude A(x) = 2x½+δ/|ρ|. Take as the band Schoenfeld's RH bound B(x) = √x log²x/(8π). His argument also bounds the remaining on-line zeros plus truncation by B(x)(1 + o(1)): a deterministic envelope on the masking.

Phase is never the bottleneck: off-line zeros have γ > 3·1012 (RH is verified to that height), so the wave passes through about 3·1011 periods inside [X, 2X]. Detection is certain once A > 2B:

log X* = (1/δ)·[ log(|ρ|/8π) + 2 log log X* ]·(1 + o(1))

In this envelope model the factor 2 is sharp. Smoothing (the ramp-weighted G) removes the log² and the truncation term but divides the signal by another γ, and comes out worse here.

For finite windows: certification needs a deterministic bound on the masking term, here from structure (the other zeros are on the line). Without it, two off-line zeros with nearly equal γ could cancel over any window shorter than about 2π/|γ1 − γ2| in log x. And exact prime counts reach only about 1029, far short of even 1037, which is why RH is tested by computing zeros.

Schoenfeld's constant was not re-derived with the quartet removed; the "(1 + o(1))" is where a small explicit adjustment would go. Effective Ω-results (for example by Pintz) give fully explicit versions.
